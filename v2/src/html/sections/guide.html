The Recursive Innovation Field Guide

Seven short chapters that give policy makers, decision makers, teachers and students one shared vocabulary before they use the tools. Practice guidance, not research findings: for evidence, use the Evidence library and primary sources.

The guide is written in English; the interface around it is translated.

Every solution is a hypothesis about the world.Treat it that way, and failure becomes information instead of embarrassment.
Why “recursive”?

Most plans are written once and judged once. A recursive approach treats every project as a loop: you act, you record what actually happened (including what failed), and that record becomes the starting point for the next pass. Each pass changes the questions, not just the answers.

Three rules for the loop

  1. Write the prediction before the result. It is the only way to be surprised honestly.
  2. Record nulls and corrections as results. “Nothing changed” and “we were wrong” are information; the ledger has entry types for both.
  3. Shrink the step until a mistake is cheap and reversible. Large irreversible steps need more evidence and more people.

In this workspace, innovation genomes capture the idea (problem, mechanism, constraints, indicators), projects capture what happened, and the ledger records decisions and corrections.

For decision makers: decision hygiene

Good decisions are rarely the product of a single brilliant analysis. They come from asking the same dull questions every time, in writing, before commitments harden. Seven are enough to start:

  1. What decision does this inform, and who owns it?
  2. What are the options, including doing nothing?
  3. What evidence do we have, how strong is it, and what is missing?
  4. Who is affected, who bears the cost, and who is not in the room?
  5. What would make us stop or change course? (Decide this in advance.)
  6. How could this be flipped into harm: surveillance, coercion, exclusion, extraction?
  7. When will we review, and how will we publish what did not work?

If you hear… ask…

If you hearAsk
“It worked in the pilot.”Compared with what? For whom? For how long? Would it work at ten times the size?
“Everyone agrees.”Who is “everyone”? Who is not here, and who would be affected if they disagreed?
“The model says…”Which inputs? What range? Which input matters most, and what would change the answer?
“We will fix it later.”What is the cheapest way to make this reversible now?
“Activity is up.”Is anyone better off, and how would we know?

Common traps

Anchoring on the first option; confusing activity with impact; averaging away real disagreement; treating a model’s range as a forecast; assuming a small pilot scales; and letting a measure become a target, after which it often stops measuring what you cared about. The decision matrix exposes how sensitive a ranking is to your own weights; the brief studio will not let the “limits and unknowns” section disappear.

For teachers and students: inquiry with guardrails

Students are not test subjects; they are co-investigators. A good classroom challenge starts with a real question sized for a class, gives every student a role that matters, and keeps the people involved safe.

  • Roles: question keeper, evidence finder, skeptic (looks for what could be wrong), data steward (keeps numbers and consent tidy), communicator. Rotate them.
  • Safety and consent: no personal information about other people; adult approval before involving anyone outside the class; permission before photos or recordings; never test anything that could hurt someone.
  • Assessment that helps: look at the question, the use of evidence, the handling of mistakes and the reflection, not only the answer. A well-documented “nothing changed” is a method success.
  • Whose data it is: a student’s reflections are theirs. In this workspace the passport stays on the device; a teacher sees only what the student chooses to hand in, under a nickname, and there is deliberately no ranking or total score.
  • Age and settings: choose the Student lens or turn on youth-safe mode on shared and school devices. It switches off internet rooms and analytics.

Open classroom mode

Evidence without fiction

Four different statements often get mixed together: what was observed, what is inferred, what is a hypothesis and what has been decided. Label each. Most bad reports are good observations wearing the clothes of a decision.

Ask a question evidence can answer

A workable question names a population, an action, a measure, a comparison and a time window:

Among [who], does [action] change [measure] compared with [alternative] over [time]?

Example: “Among grade 7 students at our school, does a weekly produce table change the share of lunches that include vegetables, compared with classes that do not have it yet, over six weeks?”

Before and after is not cause and effect

Seasons, other programmes, who chose to take part and plain chance also move numbers. A comparison group, a stepped roll-out or randomisation each reduce that risk; the pilot designer says which one you have chosen and warns when it is the weakest.

Noise and sample size

Small samples jump around. Estimate how many people you need before you start, and treat any interval as a statement about your inputs, not a promise about the world. The Model lab shows ranges and which inputs matter most.

Sources and silence

Prefer primary sources, note dates and conflicts of interest, and record nulls: studies and projects that find nothing are routinely never written up, which makes everything look more successful than it is. XP in this workspace recognises documented practice, never impact.

Zero-Harm and Anti-Inversion

Zero-Harm means designing so that the intended path does not hurt people, slowing down when consequences are high, asking the people affected, and handing legal, medical, structural and hazardous decisions to qualified people.

Anti-Inversion adds a second question: how could the helpful thing be flipped into its opposite? Almost every useful feature has a mirror image.

The right-hand column is how this workspace itself behaves: for example, records from other people wait in an inbox, the classroom review cannot be sorted by score, and nothing leaves the device unless you export it or connect on purpose.

The two-minute inversion drill

  1. Pick one feature of your plan.
  2. Write the worst-case flip in one sentence.
  3. Name who would be hurt, and who would never find out.
  4. Choose one safeguard and write down who checks it.
  5. Set a review date.

A checklist cannot prove safety; it can only make blind spots harder to keep. When in doubt, choose the safer, clearer, more transparent and more reversible option.

Data sovereignty and local-first working

This workspace keeps your records in your browser. Nothing leaves the device unless you export a file or deliberately connect to a room, and System check lists every host this session has contacted. Local-first also means your work survives a lost connection.

  • Back up regularly. Browsers can clear storage. An encrypted backup protects a file at rest; a lost passphrase cannot be recovered by anyone.
  • Restore points are taken before imports, conflicts and risky deletes, so one mistake does not cost a project.
  • Sharing by file is easiest to control. Internet rooms reveal your IP address to relays and to the other people in the room; they are off until you consent.
  • Keep less. Collect only what you will use, state when it will be deleted, and prefer aggregates to individual records.

Communities have their own frameworks for governing their data, and they are not interchangeable. Your first cycle in 30 minutes
  1. Minutes 0–5 · Lens. Choose your lens, or load the example data to see every tool filled in.
  2. 5–10 · Question. Write one answerable question using the template in chapter 4.
  3. 10–15 · People. List the people and groups affected in Stakeholders; mark who is not yet heard.
  4. 15–20 · Pilot. In the pilot designer, set a baseline, a comparison, a consent route and a stop rule.
  5. 20–25 · Inversion drill. Run the check in Risks & anti-inversion.
  6. 25–30 · Record. Create a project and log your baseline measurement. Add a ledger entry for the decision you just made.

Then do the small thing, measure honestly, and come back to record what happened, especially if it was not what you hoped.
