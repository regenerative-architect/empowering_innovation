FIELD GUIDE · LONG READ

The Recursive Innovation Field Guide

Seven short chapters that give policy makers, decision makers, teachers and students one shared vocabulary before they use the tools. Practice guidance, not research findings: for evidence, use the Evidence library and primary sources.

The guide is written in English; the interface around it is translated.

Every solution is a hypothesis about the world.Treat it that way, and failure becomes information instead of embarrassment.
Why “recursive”?

Most plans are written once and judged once. A recursive approach treats every project as a loop: you act, you record what actually happened (including what failed), and that record becomes the starting point for the next pass. Each pass changes the questions, not just the answers.

Three rules for the loop

  1. Write the prediction before the result. It is the only way to be surprised honestly.
  2. Record nulls and corrections as results. “Nothing changed” and “we were wrong” are information; the ledger has entry types for both.
  3. Shrink the step until a mistake is cheap and reversible. Large irreversible steps need more evidence and more people.

In this workspace, innovation genomes capture the idea (problem, mechanism, constraints, indicators), projects capture what happened, and the ledger records decisions and corrections.

For decision makers: decision hygiene

Good decisions are rarely the product of a single brilliant analysis. They come from asking the same dull questions every time, in writing, before commitments harden. Seven are enough to start:

  1. What decision does this inform, and who owns it?
  2. What are the options, including doing nothing?
  3. What evidence do we have, how strong is it, and what is missing?
  4. Who is affected, who bears the cost, and who is not in the room?
  5. What would make us stop or change course? (Decide this in advance.)
  6. How could this be flipped into harm: surveillance, coercion, exclusion, extraction?
  7. When will we review, and how will we publish what did not work?

If you hear… ask…

If you hearAsk
“It worked in the pilot.”Compared with what? For whom? For how long? Would it work at ten times the size?
“Everyone agrees.”Who is “everyone”? Who is not here, and who would be affected if they disagreed?
“The model says…”Which inputs? What range? Which input matters most, and what would change the answer?
“We will fix it later.”What is the cheapest way to make this reversible now?
“Activity is up.”Is anyone better off, and how would we know?

Common traps

Anchoring on the first option; confusing activity with impact; averaging away real disagreement; treating a model’s range as a forecast; assuming a small pilot scales; and letting a measure become a target, after which it often stops measuring what you cared about. The decision matrix exposes how sensitive a ranking is to your own weights; the brief studio will not let the “limits and unknowns” section disappear.

For teachers and students: inquiry with guardrails

Students are not test subjects; they are co-investigators. A good classroom challenge starts with a real question sized for a class, gives every student a role that matters, and keeps the people involved safe.

  • Roles: question keeper, evidence finder, skeptic (looks for what could be wrong), data steward (keeps numbers and consent tidy), communicator. Rotate them.
  • Safety and consent: no personal information about other people; adult approval before involving anyone outside the class; permission before photos or recordings; never test anything that could hurt someone.
  • Assessment that helps: look at the question, the use of evidence, the handling of mistakes and the reflection, not only the answer. A well-documented “nothing changed” is a method success.
  • Whose data it is: a student’s reflections are theirs. In this workspace the passport stays on the device; a teacher sees only what the student chooses to hand in, under a nickname, and there is deliberately no ranking or total score.
  • Age and settings: choose the Student lens or turn on youth-safe mode on shared and school devices. It switches off internet rooms and analytics.

Open classroom mode

Evidence without fiction

Four different statements often get mixed together: what was observed, what is inferred, what is a hypothesis and what has been decided. Label each. Most bad reports are good observations wearing the clothes of a decision.

Ask a question evidence can answer

A workable question names a population, an action, a measure, a comparison and a time window:

Among [who], does [action] change [measure] compared with [alternative] over [time]?

Example: “Among grade 7 students at our school, does a weekly produce table change the share of lunches that include vegetables, compared with classes that do not have it yet, over six weeks?”

Before and after is not cause and effect

Seasons, other programmes, who chose to take part and plain chance also move numbers. A comparison group, a stepped roll-out or randomisation each reduce that risk; the pilot designer says which one you have chosen and warns when it is the weakest.

Noise and sample size

Small samples jump around. Estimate how many people you need before you start, and treat any interval as a statement about your inputs, not a promise about the world. The Model lab shows ranges and which inputs matter most.

Sources and silence

Prefer primary sources, note dates and conflicts of interest, and record nulls: studies and projects that find nothing are routinely never written up, which makes everything look more successful than it is. XP in this workspace recognises documented practice, never impact.

A helpful tool can be flipped into its opposite. Plan for the flip.That is what “Anti-Inversion” means.
Zero-Harm and Anti-Inversion

Zero-Harm means designing so that the intended path does not hurt people, slowing down when consequences are high, asking the people affected, and handing legal, medical, structural and hazardous decisions to qualified people.

Anti-Inversion adds a second question: how could the helpful thing be flipped into its opposite? Almost every useful feature has a mirror image.

The right-hand column is how this workspace itself behaves: for example, records from other people wait in an inbox, the classroom review cannot be sorted by score, and nothing leaves the device unless you export it or connect on purpose.

The two-minute inversion drill

  1. Pick one feature of your plan.
  2. Write the worst-case flip in one sentence.
  3. Name who would be hurt, and who would never find out.
  4. Choose one safeguard and write down who checks it.
  5. Set a review date.

A checklist cannot prove safety; it can only make blind spots harder to keep. When in doubt, choose the safer, clearer, more transparent and more reversible option.

Data sovereignty and local-first working

This workspace keeps your records in your browser. Nothing leaves the device unless you export a file or deliberately connect to a room, and System check lists every host this session has contacted. Local-first also means your work survives a lost connection.

  • Back up regularly. Browsers can clear storage. An encrypted backup protects a file at rest; a lost passphrase cannot be recovered by anyone.
  • Restore points are taken before imports, conflicts and risky deletes, so one mistake does not cost a project.
  • Sharing by file is easiest to control. Internet rooms reveal your IP address to relays and to the other people in the room; they are off until you consent.
  • Keep less. Collect only what you will use, state when it will be deleted, and prefer aggregates to individual records.

Communities have their own frameworks for governing their data, and they are not interchangeable. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, ethics) were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance as an international framework. OCAP® (ownership, control, access, possession) is a registered trademark of the First Nations Information Governance Centre and is a set of specifically First Nations principles, which each First Nation or region may interpret in its own way. Neither is a checklist to borrow: learn from them directly and work through the community’s own process. CARE Principles (GIDA) · OCAP® (FNIGC).

Your first cycle in 30 minutes
  1. Minutes 0–5 · Lens. Choose your lens, or load the example data to see every tool filled in.
  2. 5–10 · Question. Write one answerable question using the template in chapter 4.
  3. 10–15 · People. List the people and groups affected in Stakeholders; mark who is not yet heard.
  4. 15–20 · Pilot. In the pilot designer, set a baseline, a comparison, a consent route and a stop rule.
  5. 20–25 · Inversion drill. Run the check in Risks & anti-inversion.
  6. 25–30 · Record. Create a project and log your baseline measurement. Add a ledger entry for the decision you just made.

Then do the small thing, measure honestly, and come back to record what happened, especially if it was not what you hoped.

Trystero + WebRTC multiplayer

1 · One minute

Trystero helps browsers find each other; WebRTC carries application data directly between browsers after discovery. This bundle pins 0.25.3 and uses Nostr for the public room.

2 · Do this

  1. Enter the workspace to join the public commons.
  2. Open a second tab to verify BroadcastChannel fallback.
  3. For a team room, choose a room ID, strategy and optional password.
  4. Create a test task and confirm it appears on peers.
  5. If peers exchange discovery data but never connect, test a TURN fallback.

3 · Advanced

Trystero 0.25.x uses object actions: room.makeAction() returns objects with methods such as send(), while request actions support request()/requestMany(). New peers request a current snapshot; records merge by revision and retain same-revision disagreements as forks.

4 · Expert / implementation

Discovery is not the data plane. Nostr/MQTT/Torrent/IPFS exchange signaling information; application records move over WebRTC. Room passwords protect session descriptions but do not authenticate organizational identity. TURN can relay peer traffic when direct ICE paths fail. Test restrictive enterprise, carrier-grade NAT and mobile networks explicitly.

1 · One minute

2 · Do this

  1. Check the location point and service claims.
  2. Use a location challenge if the Broadband Serviceable Location is wrong or missing.
  3. Use availability challenges when a provider-reported offering cannot actually be ordered as reported.

3 · Advanced

Multidisciplinary project design

1 · One minute

Start from an observable need and outcome, then map who experiences the problem, who controls relevant resources, who has technical knowledge, who bears risk and who can independently verify results.

2 · Do this

  1. Write one falsifiable project question.
  2. Invite affected users before solution lock-in.
  3. Assign domain, implementation, data, safeguarding and review roles.
  4. Choose a reversible pilot.
  5. Predefine evidence, stop rules and a review date.

3 · Advanced

Represent the project as a dependency graph: outcomes depend on mechanisms; mechanisms depend on resources, permissions and behaviors; measurements depend on valid instruments and sampling. Identify single points of failure and cross-domain externalities.

4 · Expert / implementation

Use a responsibility model that distinguishes decision rights from advisory input. Record dissent and competing hypotheses. For coupled systems, run scenario/sensitivity analysis rather than a single forecast and design monitoring to distinguish leading indicators from outcome measures.

Evidence + provenance

1 · One minute

Every important claim should answer: what is claimed, for whom, when, from which source, by what method, with what uncertainty, and what evidence could change the conclusion?

2 · Do this

  1. Prefer primary sources.
  2. Record publication and observation dates separately.
  3. State population and geography.
  4. Capture limitations and contrary evidence.
  5. Hash exports for integrity checks, but use signatures when authorship matters.

3 · Advanced

Model provenance as edges among entity, activity and agent: a record was generated by an activity; an activity used evidence; an agent performed or reviewed the activity. Preserve revisions and negative findings.

4 · Expert / implementation

Cryptographic hashes detect byte-level change; they do not prove who authored content or whether a claim is true. For high-assurance exchange, add signatures, key-management policies, revocation and governance around who is authorized to attest to what.

Innovation genome exchange

1 · One minute

An innovation genome is a portable structured hypothesis package: problem, mechanism, evidence, method, constraints, metrics, risks, provenance and revision history.

2 · Do this

  1. Create a genome from a real problem.
  2. Run competing methods rather than one favored idea.
  3. Export JSON before major revisions.
  4. Exchange through the collaboration room.
  5. Fork unresolved conflicts and compare measured outcomes.

3 · Advanced

Segment prompt chains by entity/sub-entity so a municipality, school, family, researcher and supplier receive different responsibilities while sharing the same evidence and outcome model.

4 · Expert / implementation

Recursive innovation should optimize evidence-adjusted outcomes, not prompt volume. Add stopping criteria, novelty checks against prior art, risk budgets, replication gates and explicit mechanisms for deleting or retracting unsafe or invalid variants.

Who contributes what?

Different entities should not be flattened into identical workflows. The operating system keeps shared goals interoperable while segmenting decision rights, evidence duties and implementation responsibilities.

Families / residents

Define lived needs, constraints, accessibility, acceptability and household outcomes. Control consent for personal data and participation.

Educational bodies

Learning design, apprenticeships, evaluation, student safeguarding, research partnerships and facilities as community anchors.

Businesses

Operational feasibility, local supply, maintenance, workforce pathways, customer evidence and rapid pilot execution.

Corporations

Capital equipment, large supply chains, engineering capacity, standards participation and scaled procurement—subject to transparent conflicts of interest.

Governments / public agencies

Public data, infrastructure coordination, procurement, statutory permissions, service delivery and accountable use of public funds. This tool does not replace official process.

Research institutions

Study design, causal inference, uncertainty analysis, reproducibility, independent evaluation and long-horizon knowledge stewardship.

Nonprofits / community groups

Trust-building, outreach, mutual aid, inclusion, local governance, volunteer coordination and feedback from populations institutions may miss.

Cross-entity coalition

Maintains the shared problem definition, dependency graph, evidence ledger, conflict register, milestone cadence and decision log across all contributors.

Technical depth · research-to-action mathematics, provenance, security, and workflow

Recursive design: Gt+1 = revise(Gt, Qt, Mt, Et), where G is a versioned genome, Q questions, M candidate methods and E externally acquired evidence or observations. Generated questions are not E. Loop termination: bounded cycles, unresolved safety gates, or missing human authorization.

Identification: evaluate intervention outcomes against a counterfactual when feasible; descriptive change alone does not establish causality. Specify indicators, denominator, measurement error, distribution and adverse effects in advance. Prefer reversible pilots, independent critique, and situated expertise.

Exchange semantics: packet schema v2; revision, origin/fork lineage, event dates and source references. W3C PROV concepts (entity, activity, agent) inspire the provenance structure, but this file does not claim to generate fully conformant PROV-O RDF. An exported JSON Schema describes a subset of packet structure; the built-in validator enforces additional domain and length rules. No arbitrary code from imported JSON is executed.

Zero harm / anti-inversion: participants retain refusal and exit rights; incentives never substitute for informed consent. Data minimization, human review, conflict visibility and safe failure take precedence over maximization of automation.

OWN YOUR INFORMATION
Backup, import, export and privacy
You can always leave with a copy, go back to an earlier state, or erase everything.


Guide · backup and privacy
What: Portable, versioned local files. How: Export a full private backup for your own device (optionally encrypted with a passphrase) or a redacted summary for sharing. Import only trusted, readable JSON files. Outcome: You keep an independent copy. Browser storage can be cleared; an exported backup is strongly recommended. Plain JSON backups are NOT encrypted; use the encrypted option for anything sensitive.




Export workspace
Default exports redact project descriptions, task descriptions and observation notes, rotate record IDs, and omit genome records and toolkit records that were not marked shareable. A full backup contains private text, so store it securely. Numbers and dates can still be identifying; review before sharing.
Encrypted backups use a passphrase you choose (PBKDF2 + AES-GCM in your browser). If you lose the passphrase, nobody, including us, can recover the file. Redacted exports are REDACTED, not guaranteed anonymous.


Import workspace
Schema v1, v2 and v3 files are accepted. A restore point is saved before every import. Imported text is validated, size-limited and rendered as text, never as HTML.


Restore points
Automatic snapshots are taken before imports, conflicts and every few minutes of editing. They live in this browser only.


Storage & persistence
Your v3 data was copied into v4 and left untouched. Delete the old copies once you have verified your workspace.


Data lifecycle
  • No cookies, trackers, hidden telemetry or remote API calls. Optional local-only counters are off by default and viewable in System check.

Install & offline cache (hosted copies only)
